Timing a purchase and a sale to close on the exact same day rarely works
out perfectly. If you want to buy your next home before your current one
sells, there are a handful of established ways to bridge the gap — each
with real tradeoffs.

## Option 1: A sale-contingent offer

You make an offer on the new home that's contingent on your current home
selling first. It's the lowest-risk option financially, since you're
never carrying two mortgages, but it's also the weakest offer in a
competitive market — sellers often prefer offers without that
contingency, especially when multiple offers are on the table.

## Option 2: A bridge loan

A short-term loan secured against the equity in your current home, used
to cover the down payment (or full purchase) on the new one before your
old home sells. It lets you make a stronger, non-contingent offer, but
bridge loans typically carry higher interest rates and fees than a
standard mortgage, and you're relying on your current home selling in a
reasonably predictable timeframe to pay it off.

## Option 3: A HELOC or home equity loan on your current home

Similar goal to a bridge loan — access equity in your current home before
it sells — but structured as a more conventional home equity product,
which can sometimes mean better terms than a bridge loan specifically.
This generally needs to be set up *before* your current home is listed,
since some lenders are hesitant to open a new equity line on a home
that's actively for sale.

## Option 4: Carry two payments temporarily

If your finances support it, you simply close on the new home while
still owning (and paying for) the old one, then sell the old home soon
after. This avoids contingency and bridge-financing complexity, but it
requires qualifying for both mortgages simultaneously from a
debt-to-income standpoint, which isn't realistic for every buyer.

## Option 5: A rent-back agreement, in reverse

Less common, but some buyers negotiate to close on their current home's
sale first, then rent it back from the new buyer for a short period
while they finalize the purchase of their next home. This avoids
carrying two mortgages but adds moving logistics and depends on a buyer
willing to agree to it.

## A quick comparison

| Option | Carries two payments? | Offer strength | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sale-contingent offer | No | Weaker | Losing the new home to a non-contingent offer |
| Bridge loan | Briefly | Strong | Higher cost; relies on timely sale |
| HELOC on current home | Briefly | Strong | Must set up before listing |
| Carry both payments | Yes | Strong | Qualifying for both, cash flow strain |
| Reverse rent-back | No | Strong | Depends on buyer's willingness |

## Frequently asked questions

**Which option is cheapest?**
A sale-contingent offer avoids extra financing costs entirely, but it
comes at the cost of a weaker offer — there's a real tradeoff between
cost and competitiveness, not a single best answer for every buyer.

**Can I qualify for a new mortgage while I still owe on my current
one?**
Often yes, depending on your income, debt-to-income ratio, and the
specific loan program — but it's not automatic, and it's worth getting
pre-approved with your actual numbers before assuming it'll work.

**Do I need to decide on one approach before I start house hunting?**
It helps to have a plan in mind and talk it through with a lender and
agent early, since some options (like a HELOC on your current home) need
to be arranged before you list, not after an offer is already in motion.
