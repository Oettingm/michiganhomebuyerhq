Most first-time buyers plan around their down payment and forget that
several other cash requirements land on the same day. The total is called
**cash to close**, and it's usually larger than people expect.

## The four pieces of cash to close

### 1. Down payment
This ranges from 0% (VA and some USDA loans, if eligible) to 3% (many
conventional programs) to 3.5% (FHA) to 20%+ if you're avoiding mortgage
insurance.

### 2. Closing costs
Typically 2-5% of the purchase price. Covers lender fees, title work,
appraisal, and Michigan's transfer taxes. (See the full closing costs
breakdown for the details.)

### 3. Prepaid items
First year of homeowners insurance, and several months of property tax
and insurance held in escrow. This isn't optional — it's required by
almost every lender.

### 4. Earnest money
A deposit you put down when your offer is accepted, usually 1-2% of the
purchase price, to show you're serious. This isn't an extra cost — it
gets credited toward your down payment or closing costs at closing — but
it needs to be available as cash earlier in the process, before closing
day.

## A simplified example

On a $275,000 home with a 3.5% FHA down payment:

| Item | Estimated amount |
|---|---|
| Down payment (3.5%) | $9,625 |
| Closing costs (~3%) | $8,250 |
| Prepaid insurance/tax escrow | $2,000–$3,500 |
| **Total cash to close (approx.)** | **$20,000–$21,500** |

Earnest money (say $3,000) is part of that total, not stacked on top of
it, assuming it's applied at closing as expected.

## Ways this number can come down

- Seller-paid closing costs (negotiated as part of your offer)
- Down payment assistance programs like MSHDA
- Gift funds from family, allowed on most loan types with proper
  documentation
- Lender credits, usually in exchange for a slightly higher interest rate

## Frequently asked questions

**Is earnest money the same as my down payment?**
No, but it typically gets applied toward it. Earnest money is paid
earlier, when your offer is accepted; it's credited at closing rather
than being a separate additional cost.

**Can all of my cash to close come from a gift?**
Often yes, though the specific rules on how much and from whom vary by
loan program — this is worth confirming directly with your lender for
your specific loan type.

**Do I need cash to close in a checking account, or can it be in
investments?**
Lenders typically want to see funds that are readily accessible and can be
verified through statements; funds sitting in retirement accounts or
volatile investments sometimes require extra documentation or aren't
countable the same way.
